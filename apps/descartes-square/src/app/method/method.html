The method

Descartes' Square

The four-question technique — how to fill it in, why it works, and a worked example in full.

What it is

A decision-making technique in which you look at one and the same choice from four angles instead of the usual two. Instead of a list of pros and cons, you fill in four cells:

If I do it If I don't

What appears

1 If I do it What I gain
2 If I don't What I keep

What disappears

3 If I do it What I risk
4 If I don't What I miss

The classic phrasings run like this:

    • }

Double negatives are hard to hold in your head, so they are put in plainer language above — the meaning is the same.

All the power of the method is in the bottom row. Thinking a decision through, most people automatically work with only the top half of the square. The bottom half is about cost — and not only the cost of acting, but the cost of doing nothing, which we almost never count.

You need 30–40 minutes, quiet, and honesty.

Why it works

The method looks too simple to change anything. But each cell closes off a specific trap in thinking.

"Should I…" narrows the field.

In "Decisive", Chip and Dan Heath cite Paul Nutt's research: organisational decisions framed as "whether or not" failed roughly twice as often as those where several options were on the table. The phrase "should I" is itself a signal that you are standing on one patch of the field. The square won't invent a third option for you, but it will shake the illusion that the choice is binary.

Doing nothing feels free.

Doing nothing feels like the neutral option — in fact it is a decision too, just one with a deferred bill. Research by Gilovich and Medvec showed a consistent pattern (though not an iron-clad one — later replications found a weaker effect): we regret actions more sharply straight away, and inactions for longer. The "what I'll miss if I don't" quadrant puts that bill in front of you now, rather than in five years.

We are smarter about other people's business.

In their own situation a person reasons worse than in an identical situation belonging to someone else — but the difference disappears if you look at yourself from a distance. Writing things down is a primitive form of that distance: a thought moved onto paper stops being you and becomes an object.

How to fill it in

  1. State the decision as one concrete action.

    Not "something about work", but "resign before the end of September". A vague statement gives vague answers in all four cells.

  2. Go in the order 1 → 4 → 2 → 3.

    Easiest first — the gains from acting. Then straight to the hardest and most useful — the losses from not acting. That order stops you gutting the bottom half once you are already tired.

  3. At least five points in each cell.

    The first two or three are what you already knew. The valuable part starts at the fourth.

  4. Write facts, not feelings.

    "It'll be scary" is not a point. "For the first 3–4 months income will be about 30% lower" is a point. If there is no specificity, that often means you are short on information, not on nerve.

  5. Don't count the points.

    The square is not a calculator. Three points in one cell can outweigh twelve in another. You are not looking for a majority — you are looking for the thing you are not prepared to live with.

An example

The decision

Take the offer from another company. The current job is stable, the team is good, growth has stopped. The offer is +25% on salary, an unfamiliar product, less predictability.

1 What I gain if I move

  • Salary a quarter higher.
  • A new domain area that isn't on my CV.
  • Experience onboarding into someone else's codebase — a skill I haven't trained in four years.
  • A formally more senior title.

4 What I lose if I don't move

  • Another year in a role I have already outgrown.
  • Market value: in four years my CV has gained one line.
  • This particular offer — there aren't many like it in the domain.
  • Probably my motivation: for the last six months I have been working at exactly the minimum.

2 What I keep if I don't move

  • A team I am comfortable in.
  • A reputation I built over four years.
  • A predictable schedule.
  • The chance to see a project that matters to me through to release.

3 What I risk if I move

  • The company is younger — there is a chance it won't survive the year.
  • For the first three months I will look incompetent.
  • The promised freedom may turn out to be chaos, and you can't see that in an interview.
  • I don't know my future manager, and that is the single biggest factor in job satisfaction.

What to do with this.

The hardest thing here isn't the pros against the cons, it is two points: "I don't know the manager" and "the freedom may turn out to be chaos". Neither is about courage — both are about missing information. The square didn't say what to do. It said that before deciding, it is worth asking for one more conversation — with the future manager, and with someone who has already worked there a year.

This is the typical result. The square rarely delivers a verdict — far more often it shows you what you haven't yet found out. And after that the decision often becomes obvious.

Common mistakes

Phrasings along the lines of "should I be happier".

The square works on an action, not on a desired state.

The same point in different cells.

If "I'll be left without stability" sits in both the risks and the losses, you are writing one thought twice.

Filling it in for confirmation.

If you have already decided, the square becomes your decision's advocate. The tell: the bottom half is filled in more briefly, and reluctantly.

Filling it in at an emotional peak.

Under heavy stress or euphoria the square records your state, not your situation. Better to put it aside for a day and reread it.

When the square isn't the right tool

You don't need it for reversible decisions: if a mistake can be fixed in a week, it is cheaper just to try. It works poorly for choosing between three or more options — there you need criteria and a comparison table. And it doesn't replace professional help on medical, legal or financial questions: it is a tool for preparing for the conversation with a specialist, not an alternative to it.
